Denser on-chip memory for RV328: options on GF180MCU

Research report, 20 November 2026. Nothing in this report has been placed and routed, and nothing was simulated at transistor level. Every number is marked as one of:

  • measured: taken by me from files in the installed PDK (process design kit) or the project, with the method in "What I measured";
  • cited: stated by a source, with its URL in "Sources";
  • estimate: my own arithmetic on measured numbers, for something that does not exist yet.

Summary and recommendation

The foundry memory is sparse because of its surroundings, not its storage cell. In the 512 x 8 macro the cells that store bits take 26 % of the area; the other 74 % is the row decoder, the read and write circuits and the guard ring (measured). Thirty-one small macros pay that overhead thirty-one times.

There is a denser memory already in the installed PDK, which the project does not use: the 3.3 V macros of library gf180mcu_ocd_ip_sram, redrawn from the foundry macro by Tim Edwards (Open Circuit Design). The largest, 1024 x 8, holds twice the bits of our macro in three quarters of its area: 19.0 um2 per bit against 51.1, which is 2.7 times denser (measured). It has the same pin arrangement as the foundry macro. With it, the whole 32 KB program plus the 2 KB RAM is 34 macros and 5.28 mm2, less than the 6.49 mm2 the 31 macros take today (measured macro areas, no routing channels). It was made on the first wafer.space run, and its author's test notes and conference abstract say it works (cited). But it needs a 3.3 V supply on a 5 V chip, and its timing files are copies of the 5 V macro's files, not a characterisation (measured). A second pass (next section) simulated the macro at transistor level: it works at three corners and answers in 3.2 ns at the slow corner without wiring, an estimated 5 to 6 ns with it, and draws 0.7 to 0.9 mA when read at 20 MHz, more with wiring. The open cost is a regulator without external capacitor and 3.5 to 7 nF of capacitance on the chip (estimate).

Recommendation, in three steps.

  1. First silicon: keep the 5 V foundry macro. It is the only memory here with foundry timing at our corner (slow silicon, 4.5 V, 125 C: output 10.3 ns after the clock, shortest cycle 11.9 ns, measured from the liberty file) and it needs no second supply. The run 3 deadline is 16 December 2026 (cited); no denser option can be adopted and verified by then. Do not make the tape-out wait for memory.
  2. Next, try the cheapest gain, which needs no new memory at all: a read-only library built from ordinary logic cells. In the benchmark firmware 13.5 KB of 19.1 KB is library code (C library and software floating point); only 5.7 KB is the application (measured from the linker map). Synthesised as logic, the complete 19.4 KB firmware image is 0.31 mm2 of cells, 2.0 um2 per bit, against 8.2 mm2 for the same bytes in foundry SRAM (measured by synthesis only: no buffering, no placement, and the unbuffered netlist does not meet 50 ns). A library in such a ROM would leave the cache holding only application code. This changes what the chip is: the library is frozen at tape-out and the tool chain must link against it. It is worth one experiment: a ROM block through the flow, and the cache study repeated with the library outside the cache.
  3. For the whole program on the chip, plan a 3.3 V memory island with the gf180mcu_ocd_ip_sram 1024 x 8 macro, for a second silicon. It needs a voltage regulator in the analog macro, level shifters, a second supply net in the flow, and real timing for the macro. The second pass below gives it a "go, if" verdict and lists the next steps; the author's measurements were presented in September 2026 and a second test chip is being delivered now.

Not recommended: memories from flip-flops or latches (2 to 3 times worse than the foundry macro, measured cell areas), eFuse (87 to 166 um2 per bit and one-time), OpenRAM on GF180MCU (experimental, no macro, no silicon), and building our own 5 V array from the foundry's cells (about 2 times denser by estimate, but a custom-memory project with no silicon proof; reconsider it only if the 3.3 V island is ruled out).

The half-size slot is out of reach by memory density alone. With the 3.3 V macros the present contents (12 KB cache, tags, 2 KB RAM) take 2.47 mm2; with 1.88 mm2 of logic cells and the 1.30 mm2 analog block that is 5.65 mm2 in a 5.02 mm2 core (estimate from measured areas).

The 3.3 V memory, second pass

Added 20 November 2026 after the first pass, for option 1b (the gf180mcu_ocd_ip_sram 1024 x 8 macro). New here: transistor-level simulation of the macro's own netlist. The same marks apply: measured (here that includes "simulated by me"), cited, estimate. The work was cut short; what was not done is listed as not done. Verdict at the end of the section.

In short. In simulation the macro works at all three corners and is fast: at slow silicon, 3.0 V and 125 C the output follows the clock by 3.2 ns without wiring and by an estimated 5 to 6 ns with it, against 10.3 ns for the 5 V foundry macro. One access takes 36 to 45 pC from the supply without wiring, about 1.75 times that with it. A level shifter that is already drawn inside the 3.3 V pad library adds 1.0 to 1.9 ns. Nothing found so far kills the idea. What remains is engineering that this project has not done before: a regulator without an external capacitor, a few nanofarads of decoupling, and a second supply in the flow.

1. Does it work and how fast (simulation)

What was simulated.

  • The PDK ships one SPICE netlist per macro. It is the schematic exported from xschem (its first line names the schematic file): transistors with their own junction areas, no wiring capacitance or resistance. The PDK ships no extracted netlist (measured: file list).
  • To see what wiring does, I extracted the 64 x 8 and 1024 x 8 layouts from the PDK's Magic files myself: flattened, with capacitance between wires and to the substrate, without wire resistance. A hierarchical extraction was unusable (it contains large negative correction capacitors). Only the 64 x 8 extracted netlist was simulated; the extracted 1024 x 8 run was not done.
  • Simulator ngspice 42 with the PDK models (sm141064.ngspice, sections ss, typical, ff).
  • One change to the netlist was needed. ngspice picks a transistor's model bin from its total width but evaluates it with the width of one finger; two device sizes in the macro then get an invalid model and one is wider than any bin, and the simulation fails at the first time step. I rewrote every multi-finger transistor as parallel single-finger ones with the same total junction area (fixnet.py). A check on one device gave the same current within 0.5 %.
  • Bench (bench.py): supply ramped up in 10 ns, CEN high at power-up as the author's notes require, input edges 0.5 ns, 50 fF on every output, inputs changing 5 ns before the clock unless stated. One operation per clock: write A5 to address 0, write 5A to the highest address, read both, read the first again; then the same with the two values exchanged. So every bit is written and read as 0 and as 1 at the first row and at the last row. The 1024 x 8 runs use the first half of that sequence.

  • Wire resistance is in neither netlist. It should matter little: the word line is strapped at every eighth cell (measured in the first pass), and a 390 um bit line in Metal2 is on the order of 100 ohm against about 100 fF (estimate).

What this means for the 50 ns clock. After the clock edge the path is: macro output 5 to 6 ns (estimate), level shifter 1.0 to 1.9 ns (simulated, below), then the read multiplexing and the processor's fetch path at 5 V as today. That is 6 to 8 ns where the foundry macro takes 10.3 ns, so the memory would be no slower than today's, with a margin of about 2 ns. Before the edge, the address passes one buffer on the 3.3 V rail (0.4 to 0.6 ns, simulated) and needs a few tenths of a nanosecond of set-up, where the foundry macro asks for 2.0 ns. The memory clock passes the same kind of buffer, which the clock tree has to balance.

How far to trust it. The function and the order of magnitude are solid: two netlists, three corners, two macro sizes. The absolute delay of the 1024 x 8 macro with wiring is an estimate until the extracted run is done. Nothing here covers transistor mismatch, which decides whether a sense amplifier reads a weak cell correctly; that is what silicon measurements are for.

3. Silicon evidence

What I could find about measurements on run 1 silicon (all cited unless marked):

  • The test chip's commit of 4 July 2026 says the first test chip has been tested on silicon. The one finding it reports is in the power-on reset block: working, but 14 to 16 seconds long at a 3.3 V supply, redesigned for run 2. No memory fault is reported.
  • The written test notes cover one bench set-up: one pattern generator at 3.3 V on core and pads, the 256-word macros, four data bits, six address bits, with a note that the core can also be tried at 1.8 V and that further tests with two instruments are still to do. The screenshot of 30 June 2026 shows correct counting at about 40 ns per value (my reading). I found no result for the 1024-word macro, no speed limit, no voltage or temperature sweep, and no failure report.
  • The ORConf talk of 12 September 2026 announces characterisation of the 3.3 V memory; I could not read its content (video only). Not verified.
  • The library's own history confirms the timing files: its first commit says timing has not been updated from the original, and a merged change of May 2026 by another user notes that the Verilog timing block was a verbatim copy of the 5 V model and replaces it with values from the specification tables in the repository. Those tables are the 5 V macro's (measured: the same 18.2595 ns period at 3.0 V and 125 C as the foundry liberty file). The change reports no simulation and no silicon, and points out one implausible pair of entries.
  • Other users: that same user builds a chip with 16 of these macros at a 10 MHz target; a Tiny Tapeout project carries the 256-word macro with a 50 MHz clock entry. Neither reports silicon results.
  • The foundry asked for one rule fix in the cell array (enclosed implant area) on 2 January 2026, before the masks; it was made.
  • The wafer.space "BondView" page for the test chip (20 of 50 boards passing) is about wire bonding of the boards, not about the memory (my reading; the page does not explain itself).

So: silicon exists, its author says it works, and one partial functional test is documented. Speed and margins on silicon are not verified by anything I could read.

4. The boundary between 5 V and 3.3 V

Down, 5 V into the island. No special cell is needed.

  • A cell of gf180mcu_fd_sc_mcu9t5v0 supplied from the 3.3 V rail can take a 5 V input: the input touches only gates, and the cells are built from the thick-oxide transistors (nfet_05v0, pfet_05v0 in the library netlist, measured). These cells need rows of their own with the n-well on 3.3 V.
  • Speed (simulated, buf_2, 30 fF load, 5 V swing at the input): 0.21/0.39 ns rise/fall typical at 3.3 V, 0.42/0.59 ns at slow silicon, 3.0 V, 125 C, 0.46/0.67 ns at 2.7 V. The library has liberty files for 3.0, 3.3 and 3.6 V, so the flow can time such cells; in those files the cells are about 1.4 to 1.9 times slower at 3.0 V than at 4.5 V (measured, first and last table entries of buf_2).

Up, 3.3 V into the 5 V core.

  • A plain 5 V gate will not do. Simulated: a 5 V inverter at 5.5 V with its input held at 3.0 V still gives a low output (0.2 to 0.5 V), but draws 68 to 180 uA continuously, depending on the corner. On 272 memory outputs that is up to 49 mA.
  • Level-shifter cells in the installed PDK (measured, all of libs.ref searched by cell name):
    • gf180mcu_osu_sc_gp12t3v3__lshifup and __lshifdown exist, but are built only from 3.3 V transistors, for a lower core voltage. Not usable at 5 V. (My first pass said the PDK has no level-shifter cell; that was wrong in the letter, and is corrected below.)
    • The dual-voltage pad library gf180mcu_ocd_io has the right circuit inside its pads (io_lvlshft: a 3.3 V inverter, two thick-oxide n-transistors and two cross-coupled thick-oxide p-transistors, each 1.5 um wide and 0.7 um long). Its layout cell lvlshift_up is 5.53 x 12.91 um, 71 um2, plus 12 um2 for the inverter. It is not a library cell: no LEF, no liberty file. It has been made on run 1 inside the pads of the test chip.
  • That circuit simulated alone, 30 fF load: 0.6 ns typical; 1.0 to 1.3 ns at slow silicon, 125 C, 3.0 V in, 4.5 or 5.5 V out; 1.9 ns in the worst cases tried (slow n with fast p at -40 C, or a 2.7 V input). It switched fully in all ten cases, with no static current.
  • So the smallest safe circuit is this one, redrawn as a standard cell with two wells, with LEF and a liberty file made by simulation. About 85 um2 each (measured from the pad library's cells).

How many signals. One macro has 29 inputs and 8 outputs (measured). Per macro that would be 986 inputs and 272 outputs for 34 macros. With shared buses it is far fewer (estimate):

5. The supply

  • Nothing usable exists. The installed PDK has no regulator (measured: library list). The open designs I found are unfinished: a capless 5 V to 3.3 V regulator for 10 mA from a student design challenge, at the stage of target specifications, and a 3.3 V to 1.8 V regulator that needs an external capacitor and has no layout (both cited). Neither has silicon. The 3.3 V test chips of run 1 and run 2 take their 3.3 V from pins (cited: pin list), so no silicon here proves an on-chip supply.
  • What a regulator for this island needs (estimates from small simulations):
    • Load 15 to 20 mA at most, a few microamperes in standby.
    • Pass transistor: a thick-oxide p-transistor 1000 um wide and 0.55 um long passes 16 mA with 2.0 V of gate drive and 54 mA with 3.5 V at slow silicon, 125 C, 4.5 V in, 3.3 V out (simulated). So about 1000 to 1500 um, a few thousandths of a mm2.
    • Below about 3.6 V at the pin it can only pass the supply through. To lose no more than 0.1 V at 15 mA and 3.0 V it would need about 3000 um (simulated: 5.4 mA per 1000 um). The chip promises 20 MHz only from 4.5 V, so this matters only for low-voltage operation, where the macro's own limit is also unknown.
    • Capacitance on the chip: 3.5 to 7 nF for the bursts of section 2. Gate capacitance from the models is 4.0 fF per um2 for a 3.3 V transistor and 2.1 fF per um2 for a thick-oxide one (simulated), so 0.9 to 1.75 mm2 of capacitor, less whatever the macros bring themselves (not done). This is the largest cost of the island and takes back much of the area it frees. Accepting a larger dip, or spreading the accesses, reduces it.
    • No external capacitor is possible on the 32-pin package, so the loop has to be stable with the on-chip capacitance alone over a load range of more than 1000 to 1. That is a known but not trivial design. The project's bandgap can serve as its reference.
    • Power-down: either the island stays on, with the regulator's own current and the leakage above (not near an ATmega's power-down current when hot), or it is switched off and the program is copied again on wake-up, about 30 ms. A separate low-current keeper is a third way. Not designed.

Verdict: go, if three things hold

Nothing in simulation, in the documented silicon or in the arithmetic rules the 3.3 V memory out. It is faster than the foundry macro, the boundary circuits exist as drawn silicon to copy, and it fits. It is not ready to adopt, and not for the first tape-out.

Go if:

  1. the 1024 x 8 macro still works and stays under about 8 ns with wiring at the slow corner, and the author's measurements confirm function across voltage and temperature;
  2. a regulator without external capacitor and its on-chip capacitance can be designed and fitted (the largest unknown, and the only part without any silicon example);
  3. the project accepts what power-down then costs.

Next steps, in order.

  1. Run the extracted 1024 x 8 netlist at the three corners with the existing bench (about an hour each), with the shortest cycle, set-up and hold, the standby runs with the simulator's floor lowered, and the macro's own supply capacitance. Ask the author for the characterisation shown at ORConf.
  2. Design study of the regulator and its capacitance in the project's analog flow: decide the allowed dip, then size.
  3. Draw and characterise the level-shifter cell, and try the second supply in LibreLane on a small block with two macros before touching the chip.

Third pass

Added 21 November 2026 for the 3.3 V memory (gf180mcu_ocd_ip_sram__sram1024x8m8wm1): the three conditions of the second pass's verdict, worked through one by one. Written as each item was finished; the order below is the order asked for, the verdict is at the end. Marks as before: measured (includes "simulated by me"), cited, estimate, and not done.

1. The 1024 x 8 block with its wiring

In short. With the capacitance of its layout the block works at all three corners and at 1.8 V, and answers in 5.57 ns at the slow corner (slow silicon, 3.0 V, 125 C), under the 8 ns of the condition. The first extracted run, which failed, was the fault of my extraction, not of the block.

Why the first extracted run failed (measured). That run (geometry flattened in Magic, then extracted) read 0xBF at every address and drew 26.4 mA with the clock stopped.

  • Not the bench. The extracted sub-circuit's 38 pins are in the same order as the Xdut line (VSS, VDD, A9..A0, CLK, GWEN, Q7..Q0, WEN7..WEN0, D7..D0, CEN). All 21,790 p-transistors have their body on VDD and all 38,083 n-transistors on VSS. Both supplies are connected.
  • The netlist itself. I compared it with the PDK's schematic netlist (netgen 1.5.323 with the PDK's setup file). It has 9 nets fewer than the schematic (19,757 against 19,766), and its VSS net carries 138 p-transistor source or drain terminals that the schematic's VSS does not have. The largest of them is the output stage of men, the block's internal enable. The clock generator drives men to every decoder and column through 20 p-transistors of 8.57 um (171.4 um in all). In this netlist men is VSS. So the enable never rises (every read fails), and once it should, the 171 um p-transistor connects VDD to VSS (the 26 mA).
  • The same layout extracted hierarchically (Magic 8.3.677, extract all, netlist flattened by ext2spice hierarchy off) matches the schematic in netgen: 56,808 devices and 19,766 nets on both sides, "Circuits match uniquely". So the short comes from the geometric flattening step. It is not drawn in the layout. Which feature of the flattening causes it I did not find out. Extracted alone, the clock generator and the control block are correct both ways.
  • A second fault in my new netlist: ext2spice left the substrate as a net of its own, holding 19,484 capacitors (9.6 pF of them from VDD) and no transistor. I tied it to VSS. With it floating, the slow corner gave 5.54 ns instead of 5.57 ns. The second pass's 64 x 8 netlist does not have this fault.

What was simulated. The hierarchical extraction with capacitance: 59,873 transistors and 137,377 capacitors of 0.02 fF or more, 53.9 pF in all, no negative values, no wire resistance. The bench and the multi-finger rewrite are as in the second pass. The short sequence is used: write A5 at address 0 and 5A at address 1023, then read 0, 1023, 0, with a 12 ns clock period (50 ns at 1.8 V), 50 fF on each output, and inputs changing 5 ns before the clock. One run takes 15 to 23 minutes.

2. The regulator and its capacitance

In short. One clock edge can take up to 0.68 nC from the memory rail (measured charges, worst corner). The 36 blocks bring 2.7 nF of their own to the rail. With that alone, a simple regulator keeps the rail at 2.99 V or more at every corner tried. About 2 nF more keeps it at 3.04 V or more, a first-order design and simulation result. That is roughly 1 mm2 of MIM capacitor, or 0.5 to 0.65 mm2 of MOS capacitor, against the 3.5 to 7 nF and 0.9 to 1.75 mm2 estimated in the second pass. The regulator here has a real output transistor but an idealised amplifier. It is not a design.

The load step (from the RTL and the measured charges).

  • The core is hazard3_cpu_1port (rtl/rv328_core.v): one bus. On one clock edge there is at most one access in its address phase (an instruction fetch, or a load) plus the data phase of an earlier store to RAM (ram_wr_now). A RAM read cannot meet a RAM write on the same edge; the core delays it (ram_late). The RAM's four byte blocks share one enable, so a byte store enables all four.
  • The island of the second pass (program store of 32 x 1024 x 8 as 8 banks of 4 bytes, RAM of 4 x 512 x 8): at most 8 blocks enabled on one edge, 4 read and 4 written. The other 28 blocks see the clock without being selected, unless their clocks are gated.
  • The present cache, if built from these blocks (rtl/rv328_xip.v, full layout: 3 ways of 4 KB): within a way only one bank fires per access. But all three ways are read at once, each with its tag block, so a fetch enables 15 blocks, and 19 with a store in the same edge. That is 1.29 nC at the typical corner, twice the island's. So the island, not the cache, is the case sized here.

The regulator simulated (pass3/reg2.py, ngspice, PDK models for the transistor):

  • Output stage: a native thick-oxide n-transistor (nfet_06v0_nvt, 10,000 um / 1.8 um, body on the substrate) as a source follower from VCC. A follower answers a fast load by itself: when the rail dips, its gate-source voltage grows and it conducts more, without waiting for the loop. A p-type pass transistor (the second pass's idea, and variant P of the first attempt) depends on the loop for that, and in the first attempt it lost 0.3 to 0.8 V under load.
  • Error amplifier: behavioural, a transconductor of 80 uS limited to 40 uA, 100 pF from the gate to ground, its output clamped between 0 V and VCC - 0.2 V. Reference 1.1 V (the project's bandgap), divider 2 Mohm / 1 Mohm.
  • A bleed current of 0.5 mA from the rail while awake, and 5 uA in a sleep setting (with the amplifier at 8 uS / 4 uA). The bleed keeps the follower's transconductance up, so that the step from idle to full load costs less. With 50 uA of bleed the first dip at the typical corner was 0.06 V deeper (3.053 against 3.110 V, 5 nF, earlier load estimate of 0.55 nC).

Power-down: recommendation (added 21 November, after the third pass)

The ATmega328PB keeps its program in flash and its 2 KB RAM powered: 0.2 uA in power-down (typical, 1.8 V, 25 C, watchdog off, Microchip's product page) and it resumes within microseconds to about a millisecond. Neither option above matches that: keeping the 3.3 V memory powered costs about 75 times that current at room temperature and far more hot; copying the whole program back costs about 30 ms of full power on every wake.

Recommended: switch the memory off in power-down and refill it on demand. The on-chip memory holds the whole program, with a "loaded" flag per 32-byte line. Power-up and wake from power-down clear the flags; a line is fetched from the serial memory the first time it is used (about 29 us), and since the whole program fits, a line is never thrown out. The first 2 KB (interrupt handlers and the wake-up path, already pinned in today's cache) sit in a small block that stays powered, with the regulator in a low-current sleep mode.

  • A pin or timer interrupt is answered at once; other code is slower the first time it runs after a wake and at full speed after that. A device that wakes, reads a sensor and sleeps refills only the few hundred bytes it uses.
  • Sleep current: a few uA, from the small block's leakage and the sleep regulator (estimate from the leakage simulated in the second pass; not simulated as such). Not 0.2 uA, and it should be in the datasheet as a difference from the ATmega.
  • The logic is today's cache controller with one way of the full program size: fill on miss, never evict. Not designed or simulated.
  • True "nothing to load" would need non-volatile memory on the chip, which the open kit does not offer for user programs (section 5: mask ROM and logic ROM are fixed at manufacture, eFuse is one-time).

  • What it would save (measured macro areas, no channels):
    • today's contents, 14 macros of 1024 x 8 and 3 of 512 x 8: 2.47 mm2 instead of 6.49 mm2;
    • the whole 32 KB program plus 2 KB RAM, 34 macros: 5.28 mm2. In 5 V foundry macros that would be 68 macros and 14.2 mm2, more than the core.
    • wafer.space's own table gives 108 of these macros in a full slot against 80 of the 5 V macro, or 108 KB against 40 KB (cited).
  • History and silicon (all cited):
    • Added to the PDK build in November 2025, three macros finished 18 December 2025, rule fixes in February 2026, the 64-word macro in June 2026.
    • Test chip OCD2 on wafer.space run 1 with one 1024, one 512 and two 256-word macros; wafers came back 6 April 2026.
    • The test chip's own notes describe a bench set-up for the run 1 chip that exercises the 256-word macros with four data bits and six address bits at 3.3 V. A logic-analyser picture in the same repository, dated 30 June 2026, shows the four outputs counting. By my reading of its time axis one value lasts about 40 ns (25 MHz); that is my reading of a picture, not a stated result.
    • The author's ORConf talk of 12 September 2026 announces characterisation results for the 3.3 V SRAM and says the circuits are functional. I could not read the results themselves (only the abstract and a video link).
    • A second test chip (OCD3, with all four macros, 3.3 V transistors only) went on run 2; first bare dies of that run were shipped 30 October 2026. Other designers have weighed or used the macros for run 2 (cited: one design write-up); I found no silicon report from them.
  • The timing files are placeholders (measured). The liberty file of the 3.3 V 1024 x 8 macro is the 5 V foundry 512 x 8 file of the same corner with the name, header and address width changed: same delay tables, same 18.26 ns period at 3.0 V, even the 5 V macro's area. The 3.3 V transistors are not the transistors those numbers were made for. Real timing must come from simulation of the extracted netlist or from the author's measurements.
  • What adopting it takes.
    • A 3.3 V supply on the chip. The ATmega328PB package has no spare pin, so a regulator without an external capacitor, added to the analog macro. Nothing of the kind is in the PDK libraries. It must carry the read current of the active macros, which the placeholder files do not tell.
    • Level shifters. A 5 V signal must not reach a 3.3 V gate, and a 3.3 V output is not a valid high for 5 V logic. Corrected in the second pass: the PDK has no level-shifter cell usable at 5 V. The two in gf180mcu_osu_sc_gp12t3v3 are built from 3.3 V transistors, and the right circuit exists only inside the pads of gf180mcu_ocd_io. A macro has 29 inputs and 8 outputs (measured from the LEF). The inputs can share shifters across a bank; every output needs one, unless the read multiplexing is also built at 3.3 V.
    • A second supply net in LibreLane: power grid for the island, power pins of the macros on the new net, LVS with two supplies.
    • Low supply. The ATmega328PB runs from 1.8 V and our analog blocks are designed from 2.7 V. Below about 3.5 V a regulator only passes the supply through; whether the macro works there is not known.
    • Standby current of 34 KB at 3.3 V and 125 C is not known, and it flows in power-down unless the island is switched off and reloaded on wake-up.
  • A different way to use the same macros is to build the whole core at 3.3 V with the 3.3 V cell library (gf180mcu_as_sc_mcu7t3v3) and the dual-voltage pads (gf180mcu_ocd_io), as the OCD3 test chip does (cited). That removes the level shifters inside the core but replaces the cell library, the pads and every timing result of this project. I did not look into it further.

What the whole program on the chip would mean

A static memory forgets at power-off, so the program still lives in the serial memory die and is copied at reset. By the cache study's figure of 580 clocks per 32-byte line, 32 KB takes about 594,000 clocks: 30 ms at 20 MHz (estimate), but 0.6 s if the copy ran at a 1 MHz start-up clock. After that there are no misses, no tags and no cache controller.

4. Our own array from the foundry's cells

The idea. Keep the storage cell and the leaf cells of the foundry macro (row decoder slice, column multiplexer, sense amplifier, write driver, control), and build fewer, larger arrays so the periphery is shared. The leaf cells are in the PDK as separate layout cells (measured: xdec, ypass_gate, mux821, sa, wen_wm1, din, outbuf_oe and others inside the macro GDS and as Magic files).

What it could save (estimate). I extended the macro family's own size law: fixed periphery plus 4.50 um per row, and 27.0 um per group of eight columns with its well-tap column (measured pitches).

Array (5 V cell) Size (um) mm2 um2 per bit x
1 KB, 1024 x 8 (128 rows) 432 x 773 0.334 40.7 1.25
2 KB, 2048 x 8 (256 rows) 432 x 1349 0.583 35.6 1.4
4 KB, 1024 x 32 (128 rows, 256 columns) 1080 x 773 0.835 25.5 2.0
8 KB, 2048 x 32 (256 rows, 256 columns) 1080 x 1349 1.457 22.2 2.3
32 KB in one array (512 rows, 512 columns) 1944 x 2501 4.861 18.5 2.8
limit for an endless array 15.2 3.4
  • On the chip: 12 KB of cache as three 4 KB blocks, RAM as one 2 KB block, tags unchanged: about 3.7 mm2 instead of 6.5. The whole program as four 8 KB blocks plus RAM: about 6.4 mm2, the area the memories take today.
  • These are estimates and optimistic. They assume the periphery does not grow. In a real design the word-line drivers must drive four times as many cells, the bit lines are two to four times longer, the address decoding gains bits and the self-timing of the read must be redone. Expect 10 to 25 % more area, and the 32 KB single array is not credible without a redesign of the periphery.
  • The same law for the 3.3 V cell gives 12.1 um2 per bit for a 4 KB block and 10.6 for 8 KB (estimate).

What it involves.

  • Draw the new assemblies: decoder for 128 or 256 rows, 32 column slices, wider control and power. Only adding rows is the simple part: the 3.3 V 1024 x 8 macro is the 512 x 8 with twice the rows (measured), added in December 2025 (cited). In 5 V that step brings only 1.25 times.
  • Simulate read, write and the self-timing at transistor level with extracted wiring at all corners, including cell stability with the longer bit lines.
  • DRC with the foundry deck. The storage cell uses tighter rules that the deck applies only inside the "SRAM core" marker layer (measured from the deck: rule files sram_5p0 and sram_3p3, layer 108/5). Reusing the foundry cell unchanged inside the marker is how the existing macros pass. A run 2 test chip from Chips4Makers whose 3.3 V memory blocks use these rules passed the wafer.space precheck (cited); whether wafer.space or the foundry restricts the rules to foundry cells I could not find stated.
  • LVS of the array, then LEF, liberty (from simulation) and a Verilog model for the flow.

Risks. No silicon proof; a failure in memory makes the whole chip useless; the people on this project are not memory designers; it needs its own test chip to be trusted, which costs a run.

5. Read-only and one-time-programmable storage

A chip whose program the user writes cannot keep that program in ROM or in one-time memory. These options are only for content that is the same in every chip: a boot loader or a runtime library.

eFuse

  • Library gf180mcu_re_efuse is in the installed PDK: seven macros made by an open eFuse compiler (cited), with LEF, GDS, netlists and liberty files at our corners (measured: file list).
  • Density (measured from the LEF sizes): the bare 64 x 32 array is 87 um2 per bit; the complete 1024 x 32 memory with its bus interface is 3.75 mm2, 114 um2 per bit; the small ones 132 to 166. Worse than SRAM, and each bit can be written once.
  • Silicon: an earlier version went on the first open GF180MCU shuttle (GFMPW-0) in an FPGA project, and a test chip with 2,424 eFuses was on wafer.space run 1 (cited). I found no results. The compiler's README lists open improvements to wiring and timing (cited).
  • Use for RV328: a few dozen bits of calibration or configuration (oscillator trim), not program storage.

One-time-programmable memory

The foundry DRC deck has rules for an OTP marker layer (measured), but the PDK contains no OTP cell or macro, and wafer.space's statistics list OTP as drawn by no design (cited). Not available.

Mask ROM as a custom array

  • No ROM macro exists in the PDK, and the only generator I found with a GF180MCU entry is the unverified OpenRAM configuration above.
  • Rough size of a ROM cell from the ordinary design rules in the DRC deck (gate length 0.60 um at 5 V or 0.28 um at 3.3 V, contact 0.22 um, contact to gate 0.15 um, diffusion width and space): about 0.8 um2 at 5 V and 0.5 um2 at 3.3 V, against 13.5 and 6.6 um2 for the SRAM cells. With decoders and sensing a block of 8 to 32 KB might reach 3 to 6 um2 per bit. This is a rough estimate of something nobody has drawn.
  • Same effort and risk as section 4. Not worth it while the next option exists.

ROM built from logic cells (measured by synthesis)

A ROM is a fixed truth table; the synthesis tool can build it from ordinary cells. I synthesised real RV328 code from the benchmark firmware (build/bench/full.bin) as a 32-bit-wide ROM with registered output, with Yosys and the 9-track 5 V library:

Content Bits Cell area um2 per bit
2 KB slice 16,384 0.052 mm2 3.15
8 KB slice 65,536 0.155 mm2 (0.121 in 7-track cells) 2.36 (1.85)
whole image, 19,852 bytes (19.4 KB) 158,816 0.310 mm2 1.95
  • The same 19.4 KB in foundry SRAM is 39 macros, 8.2 mm2. Even at 50 % row fill the ROM is about 0.6 mm2 (estimate).
  • Limits of this measurement. Cell area after synthesis only. No buffers were inserted: a static timing run without wires at the slow corner misses a 50 ns cycle by 55 ns, because each address bit drives thousands of gates. A real block needs buffer trees or two clock cycles per read; both add area or cost speed, and neither was tried. The area per bit depends on the content.
  • Why it matters here. In the benchmark firmware full the linker map gives 5.7 KB for the application and the project's runtime, 7.4 KB for the C library (printf, logarithm) and 6.0 KB for the compiler's arithmetic library (software floating point and 64-bit helpers): 70 % library (measured; KB of 1024 bytes as elsewhere in the project). In logger 13.5 of 15.0 KB is library. The cache study found that programs under 4 KB never reload a line with any cache of 4 KB or more.
  • What it would mean for the product.
    • The library is fixed at tape-out. A fault in it cannot be repaired except by linking a replacement into the user's program, which then goes through the cache as today.
    • The tool chain must know the ROM: a symbol file per chip revision, and a linker script that resolves library calls to it. The user sees nothing of this in an Arduino-style package. The RP2040 keeps floating-point routines in its boot ROM in the same way (from memory, not checked).
    • Which routines to include is a guess about users' programs; the benchmarks are our own.
    • The user's program stays fully programmable.

6. Anything else

  • Another process option on the same shuttle. wafer.space offers one variant (gf180mcuD, five metal layers). The 3.3 V transistors are part of it, which is what option 1b uses. There is no embedded flash, and no denser foundry memory is offered (cited: technology page lists only the macros above).
  • Dynamic memory. One- to three-transistor cells with refresh would be denser than six-transistor SRAM. I found a compiler paper for such memories but no GF180MCU cell, generator or silicon. Not credible for this chip.
  • A smaller cache instead of a denser one. Section 5's ROM library is really this: it does not make memory denser, it removes the need for most of it.
